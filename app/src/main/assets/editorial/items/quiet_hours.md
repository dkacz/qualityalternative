Most of what we remember about our own lives happens in the margins — the hours no one was watching, including us. What we call attention is usually the absence of its opposite.

The argument is simpler than it sounds. Attention is not a finite resource we spend; it is a direction we keep choosing. What changes over a life is not how much we have — it's what we've practiced pointing it at.

You can hear the objection already. Surely some things deserve attention and others do not, and surely we know which is which. Surely the difference between reading and scrolling is self-evident. I used to think so too.

Then I kept a log for a week. Not of what I read, but of the feeling when I finished. There were entries marked — in my shorthand — as yes, and entries marked empty. The surprise wasn't the ratio. It was how rarely the empty ones happened to coincide with anything I would have called useful at the time.

What I had been calling attention was, in fact, the absence of its opposite. A quieter room. A single thing in front of me.
